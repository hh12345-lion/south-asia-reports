Country evidence can help explain the conditions in which an asylum claim arises, but its relevance depends on the issues in the individual case. Information about political activity, religious identity, ethnicity, caste, gender, sexual orientation, or treatment by state authorities may provide important context. It does not necessarily establish what happened to a particular person or what they may face if returned.

For solicitors preparing South Asia asylum appeals, the key task is to connect reliable country information to the material facts and issues in dispute. Evidence should be assessed in context, with attention to its source, date, geographical scope, and relationship to the appellant's circumstances.

A country expert report may assist where general country material does not adequately address a relevant, case-specific question. Its role is to provide independent specialist analysis within the expert's field of expertise, not to replace the tribunal's assessment of the evidence as a whole.

This guide explains how legal representatives can assess the relevance of country evidence to an individual asylum claim involving Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, or Bhutan.

## What Does Relevance Mean in Country Evidence?

Country evidence is relevant when it helps address an issue that matters to the determination of the claim. The question is not simply whether a source discusses the same country or general subject as the appellant's account. It is whether the information assists with a material issue in the particular case.

For example, a report about political opposition in Bangladesh may provide useful background in a claim involving alleged political activity. However, its relevance will depend on factors such as the nature of the appellant's activities, their profile, the period concerned, the location, and the alleged interest of the authorities or other actors.

Similarly, general evidence about religious minorities in India may help explain the wider context of an individual claim. It may not, by itself, establish the treatment of a particular person in a specific locality.

Assessing relevance therefore requires more than collecting country reports. It requires a reasoned connection between the evidence and the issues that need to be considered.

## Start With the Issues in the Individual Claim

Before selecting country evidence, legal representatives should identify the material issues arising from the appellant's account and the decision under challenge.

Depending on the case, these issues may include:

- The appellant's claimed political, religious, ethnic, social, or other relevant profile.
- The nature and extent of activities said to have attracted adverse attention.
- The identity and capacity of the alleged persecutor or other source of harm.
- The location and period in which the alleged events occurred.
- The reasons given for any past threats, detention, violence, discrimination, or other adverse treatment.
- Which parts of the account are disputed and require further examination.
- The availability and practical effectiveness of state protection, where relevant.
- Whether internal relocation is raised and what factors affect its feasibility or reasonableness under the applicable legal framework.
- The potential consequences of return, considered against the relevant legal tests.

Not every issue will arise in every appeal. The purpose of identifying them is to establish what the country evidence needs to help explain, rather than treating all available material as equally important.

The relevant issues may also change during proceedings. The refusal letter, further evidence, or questions raised during preparation for a hearing may identify matters requiring closer examination.

## Match the Evidence to the Appellant's Profile

Country information should be considered in light of the individual circumstances relied upon in the claim. A source may be relevant to one aspect of an appellant's profile but have limited value in relation to another.

### Political Activity and Perceived Opposition

In a claim involving political opinion or political activity, potentially relevant material may address:

- The treatment of members or supporters of a particular political party.
- Differences in the treatment of senior figures, organisers, ordinary members, and people with limited involvement.
- The circumstances in which political activity may attract adverse attention.
- The role of local political actors and the relationship between local and national authorities.
- The significance of online activity, public demonstrations, campaigning, or activity abroad, where these matters arise on the facts.

General information about political tensions does not automatically establish that every person associated with an opposition group faces the same risk. The evidence must be considered in relation to the appellant's claimed activities, profile, visibility, location, and other material circumstances.

### Religion, Ethnicity, Caste, and Other Identity-Related Circumstances

Where a claim concerns religious or ethnic identity, caste, or another protected or relevant characteristic, country evidence may help explain patterns of discrimination, violence, social exclusion, or official response.

The analysis should avoid assuming that all members of a group experience identical treatment. Differences may exist between regions, communities, social settings, and individual circumstances.

Evidence about widespread discrimination may be relevant without necessarily establishing that the treatment reaches the applicable legal threshold in a particular case. Conversely, the absence of evidence of uniform or nationwide treatment does not automatically make individualised evidence irrelevant.

### Gender, Sexual Orientation, and Other Personal Circumstances

In claims involving gender-based harm, sexual orientation, gender identity, or other personal circumstances, the relevance of country evidence may depend on social attitudes, legal provisions, patterns of enforcement, access to protection, and the treatment of people with a comparable profile.

Care is needed when interpreting sources that describe broad social conditions. Formal legal protections may not fully explain how a law operates in practice, while reported social hostility does not necessarily establish that every individual faces the same consequences.

The evidence should be assessed in relation to the particular issues raised by the claim, without assuming that a person must conform to a generalised profile to be considered at risk.

## Consider the Location and Period Covered by the Evidence

Country conditions can vary over time and between different parts of a country. Evidence that is relevant to one location or period may not adequately explain conditions elsewhere or at a later date.

When assessing a source, consider the following questions:

- Geographical scope: Does the material address the relevant country, region, city, district, or local area?
- Time period: Does it cover the period when the alleged events occurred, the period relevant to the appeal, or the anticipated circumstances on return?
- Changes in conditions: Have political developments, changes in government, legal reforms, security events, or other developments affected the position since the source was published?
- Local differences: Does the source identify significant differences between regions or communities?
- Continuity: Is there evidence that the reported pattern continued, changed, or ceased during the relevant period?

A source should not be treated as irrelevant merely because it does not cover every aspect of the appellant's circumstances. It may still provide useful context. However, its limitations should be recognised when drawing conclusions from it.

For example, reporting about conditions in a capital city may not fully address the circumstances in a rural district. Equally, evidence of a past pattern of harm may require careful consideration if the country conditions have subsequently changed.

For claims involving Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, or Bhutan, country-specific material should be selected with attention to the relevant location, period, and issues. The fact that two cases concern the same country does not mean that the same sources will be equally relevant to both.

## Evaluate the Source, Not Just Its Conclusion

A source may appear relevant because it contains a conclusion that resembles an allegation in the appellant's account. Its evidential value, however, depends on more than whether its conclusion supports a particular proposition.

Legal representatives and experts should consider how the information was obtained, what it establishes, and what limitations apply.

### Identify the Nature of the Source

Different sources serve different purposes. Relevant material may include:

- Reports from recognised international organisations.
- Government and intergovernmental publications.
- Legislation, regulations, and relevant judicial decisions.
- Reports from credible human-rights organisations.
- Academic research and specialist country analysis.
- Credible news reporting.
- Publicly available primary material, where appropriate to the issue.

The appropriate source depends on the question being examined. Legislation may establish the wording of a legal provision, for example, but may not by itself demonstrate how that provision is enforced in practice.

Likewise, a news report may provide information about a particular incident without establishing the frequency of similar incidents across a country.

### Check the Date and Methodology

Consider when the material was published and whether the information remains relevant. A recent publication is not automatically more reliable than an older source, just as an older source is not automatically obsolete.

The source's methodology also matters. Relevant considerations may include whether it identifies its sources, explains how information was gathered, distinguishes reported allegations from established facts, and acknowledges limitations.

### Distinguish General Findings From Individual Allegations

A report describing a documented pattern of conduct may support an assessment of the wider country context. It does not necessarily verify a specific incident alleged by an appellant.

Similarly, an account of an individual incident may be relevant to the nature of a potential harm but may provide limited evidence about how common that harm is.

These distinctions help prevent country evidence from being used to establish more than the source can reasonably support.

## Distinguish General Country Conditions From Case-Specific Questions

One of the central challenges in asylum appeals is deciding whether general country material adequately addresses the particular question raised by the case.

Country information may explain the legal framework, political environment, social conditions, or reported patterns of treatment. A case may nevertheless raise a more specific question about how those conditions relate to an appellant with a particular profile.

For example, the available material might describe the treatment of political opposition supporters generally, while the case concerns a person whose alleged activities took place in a particular district and who claims to have been identified by local actors.

The general evidence may be relevant, but additional analysis could be needed to understand whether the source addresses the particular circumstances relied upon.

The same distinction can arise where a claim concerns:

- A person whose activities are limited to a particular locality.
- An individual who alleges previous adverse attention from identified actors.
- A person whose profile has changed since leaving the country.
- A claimant whose circumstances differ from those of the group discussed in general country reports.
- A proposed return to a location about which the available evidence is limited.

The existence of a gap does not automatically mean that a country expert report is required. The gap must be material to an issue in the case, and expert evidence should be considered only where it can provide relevant assistance within the expert's field of expertise.

## Assess Whether a CPIN Addresses the Specific Issue

Country Policy and Information Notes (CPINs) can provide useful summaries of country information and analysis relevant to protection claims. Their relevance should be assessed by examining what a particular note actually covers.

The title of a CPIN may indicate that it concerns the same country or subject as an appeal, but this does not establish that it addresses every issue arising from the individual facts.

When reviewing a CPIN, consider:

- Whether it addresses the appellant's claimed profile or only a broader category.
- Whether the geographical coverage is sufficiently specific.
- Whether the evidence covers the relevant period.
- Which sources underpin the findings and whether those sources address the issue in dispute.
- Whether the note identifies differences in treatment between groups or circumstances.
- Whether newer or additional material may be relevant to the question being considered.
- Whether the issue raised by the case requires analysis beyond the scope of the note.

A CPIN should not be treated as irrelevant simply because it does not answer every question in the case. Nor should it be assumed to resolve an individual issue solely because it provides a general country assessment.

Where a material issue remains unresolved, legal representatives may consider whether further country material, clarification, or expert evidence is appropriate. The decision should depend on the evidential needs of the case, rather than on a general preference for one form of evidence over another.

## Consider Whether Internal Relocation Is Relevant

Internal relocation may be a material issue where it is suggested that an appellant could avoid the claimed harm by moving to another part of the country.

The relevance of country evidence depends on the proposed location and the appellant's individual circumstances. General information about safety or living conditions elsewhere may not answer every question arising in the case.

Depending on the issues in dispute, relevant evidence may concern:

- The geographical reach and capabilities of the alleged persecutor or other source of harm.
- Whether the appellant's profile or previous activities may be known beyond the original locality.
- Differences in conditions between the proposed destination and the appellant's home area.
- Access to accommodation, employment, essential services, or family support, where relevant to the assessment.
- The practical circumstances of travel, entry, residence, or establishing a life in the proposed location.
- Personal circumstances that may affect the feasibility or reasonableness of relocation under the applicable legal framework.

These are potential lines of enquiry, not a universal checklist. The relevant considerations depend on the facts, the applicable law, and the specific relocation issue raised.

Evidence that another area has different conditions may be useful, but it should not be assumed to establish that relocation is necessarily available or appropriate for a particular appellant.

## Recognise When Additional Expert Evidence Might Be Useful

A country expert report may be useful when a material question requires specialist country knowledge that is not adequately addressed by the available evidence.

The need for such evidence should be considered carefully. A report is not necessary merely because the claim concerns a foreign country or because the parties disagree about the interpretation of country information.

Potential reasons for considering expert evidence include:

- A material gap in the available country information.
- Conflicting sources that require specialist contextual analysis.
- A need to understand the significance of a particular local, political, social, or institutional context.
- A question about how a documented pattern may relate to the appellant's stated profile.
- A need to assess the significance of developments since existing material was published.
- A specific issue about the likely reach or capacity of an alleged actor, where the available evidence does not adequately address it.

The expert's proposed role should be defined by the issue requiring assistance. An instruction that identifies a clear question and provides the relevant background is more useful than a broad request to comment on whether an appellant will be at risk.

The expert must remain independent and should identify the sources, reasoning, uncertainties, and limitations relevant to their conclusions. They should not determine disputed facts that fall to the tribunal to assess or offer conclusions outside their expertise.

## Prepare Focused Instructions for a Country Expert

Where expert evidence is being considered, the instruction should identify the material issues and explain why existing evidence may not adequately address them.

Solicitors may find it helpful to provide:

- The relevant procedural documents and the decision under challenge.
- A clear summary of the appellant's account, identifying disputed and undisputed matters where possible.
- The appellant's relevant personal profile and the activities or events relied upon.
- Details of the relevant locations and dates.
- The country information already considered, including relevant CPINs.
- The specific questions that require specialist country analysis.
- Any proposed internal-relocation issue, if relevant.
- The procedural context and any applicable directions concerning expert evidence.

Instructions should not require an expert to adopt a particular conclusion. They should allow the expert to identify where the evidence supports a finding, where it is inconclusive, and where reliable information is unavailable.

## Review the Evidence as a Whole

Country evidence should be considered alongside the other material in the appeal. Its relevance and weight cannot always be assessed in isolation.

The evidence may need to be considered alongside the appellant's statement, interview record, supporting documents, witness evidence, refusal reasons, and any other material relevant to the disputed issues.

A source that provides strong evidence of a general country pattern may have limited application to a particular fact. Conversely, a source with a narrower geographical or subject focus may be highly relevant to a specific issue.

Where sources differ, the differences should be examined rather than resolved simply by counting how many sources support each position. Their authority, methodology, date, scope, and relationship to the question should all be considered.

It is also important to distinguish between a lack of evidence and evidence that a particular event or risk does not exist. The significance of an evidential gap depends on the issue, the sources available, and the limits of what can reasonably be established.

Ultimately, the decision-maker must assess the evidence in accordance with the applicable legal framework. Country experts can assist with relevant specialist questions, but their reports do not replace the tribunal's assessment of the claim.

## A Practical Relevance Review for Solicitors

Before relying on country material or commissioning further expert evidence, solicitors may find it useful to ask the following questions:

1. What issue does this source address? Identify the material fact or question to which the evidence relates.
2. How does it relate to the appellant? Consider the individual's profile, activities, location, and other relevant circumstances.
3. Is the source sufficiently specific? Check whether its geographical and subject coverage is appropriate.
4. Is the information current enough for the question? Consider publication dates and any material developments since publication.
5. What does the source actually establish? Separate documented findings, reported allegations, analysis, and inference.
6. Are there relevant limitations or conflicting sources? Identify uncertainties and avoid treating a single source as conclusive without examining its basis.
7. Does the material address the disputed issue adequately? If not, identify the precise gap rather than assuming that more evidence is automatically required.
8. Would specialist expertise materially assist? Consider whether an expert could provide relevant analysis beyond what is reasonably available from existing material.
9. Are the conclusions appropriately limited? Ensure that neither country sources nor expert opinions are treated as establishing matters beyond their evidential scope.

This review can help legal representatives focus on the evidence that matters to the particular appeal, while avoiding unnecessary duplication and overstatement.

## Conclusion

Assessing the relevance of country evidence to an individual South Asia asylum claim requires a clear connection between the material, the appellant's circumstances, and the issues that need to be determined.

General country reports, CPINs, primary sources, and specialist analysis can each contribute to that assessment. Their value depends on their reliability, scope, date, and ability to address the question under consideration.

Where existing material leaves a material question unresolved, further research or country expert evidence may be appropriate. Any additional evidence should have a defined purpose, address the individual issues in the case, and remain within the limits of what the source or expert can reasonably establish.

For solicitors preparing appeals involving Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, or Bhutan, a focused approach to country evidence can support a clearer, more transparent presentation of the issues without overstating what the evidence proves.
